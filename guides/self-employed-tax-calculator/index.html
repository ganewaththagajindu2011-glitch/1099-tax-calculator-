How taxes work for a sole proprietor

A sole proprietor generally reports business income and expenses on Schedule C. The profit from Schedule C flows into the individual federal tax return. If net earnings from self-employment are high enough, Schedule SE is used to figure self-employment tax.

The IRS says self-employment tax is made up of Social Security and Medicare taxes. The regular self-employment tax rate consists of 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare, subject to applicable limits and rules. Additional Medicare Tax can apply at higher income levels and is not modeled by this calculator.

Self-employed tax example

Suppose a sole proprietor expects $90,000 of business revenue and $20,000 of eligible business expenses. Their starting business profit estimate is $70,000. A self-employed tax estimator can use that profit, filing status, and its published assumptions to estimate federal income tax and self-employment tax.

The result is a planning estimate, not a completed tax return. Actual tax can change because of W-2 wages, other income, credits, itemized deductions, Qualified Business Income rules, retirement contributions, self-employed health insurance, state taxes, or other facts.

Do sole proprietors need quarterly estimated taxes?

Many self-employed people receive income with no federal tax withheld. The IRS says individuals including sole proprietors generally need estimated tax payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when the return is filed, subject to the detailed rules and exceptions in Form 1040-ES and Publication 505.

Your calculator savings target and your required IRS installment are not necessarily the same number. Read our 2026 quarterly tax guide before deciding how much and when to pay.

What counts as a business expense?

Software, supplies, qualifying home-office costs, eligible vehicle expenses, professional services, and other ordinary and necessary business costs may be deductible depending on the facts and tax rules. See our 1099 tax deductions guide for practical examples and recordkeeping reminders.

1099 income versus sole-proprietor income

Receiving a Form 1099-NEC is common for independent contractors, but the form itself does not define all of your tax obligations. A sole proprietor can have business income whether or not every payment is reported on a 1099. The important step is reporting business income and expenses correctly and determining the resulting net profit.

When this calculator may be too simple

Use extra care if you have significant W-2 wages, multiple businesses, large investment income, tax credits, QBI issues, retirement contributions, self-employed health-insurance deductions, itemized deductions, state or local taxes, or Additional Medicare Tax. Those items can materially change the final result.
